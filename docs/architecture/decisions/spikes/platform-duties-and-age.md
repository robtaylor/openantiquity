# Spike — What legal duties apply to a UK service where members aged 16 and over post content about possible archaeological sites?

**Status:** Resolved (2026-11-10) — PARTIAL; the service was in scope of the Online Safety Act and no law read barred 16 and 17 year olds, but children's duties applied and the scope of the 2027 under-16 rules was undefined

## Question

Can a UK service that admits contributors aged 16 and over, and publishes their posts about possible archaeological sites, meet its legal duties without age verification and without breaking heritage law?

## Why this is in question

[Decision 0009](../0009-contributors-are-16-or-over-and-there-is-no-private-messaging.md) chose a minimum contributor age of 16 and no private messaging, against a research recommendation of 18. [Decision 0005](../0005-sensitive-findings-are-generalised-by-viewer.md) chose to coarsen locations by viewer. Both rest on what the law required of such a service and what only guidance or practice suggested. The two had to be kept apart, because the research could not read some publishers' guidance at all.

## Approach

A research pass on 2026-11-10 read the statute on legislation.gov.uk, Ofcom's published guidance, gov.uk policy papers, and heritage bodies' own policies. It labelled each finding LAW, GUIDANCE, PRACTICE or POLICY. Three publishers refused scripted access: finds.org.uk (Cloudflare managed challenge), historicengland.org.uk (Cloudflare managed challenge) and trove.scot (Azure WAF JavaScript challenge). A retry with a browser user agent was stopped by the session's permission system as bot-check circumvention and was not pursued. Claims about those three rest on search snippets, a PAS leaflet hosted by NCMD and the HES corporate site.

- **Q1.** Is the service in scope of the Online Safety Act, and what does it owe?
- **Q2.** What does the law say about children, consent age and the 2027 under-16 rules?
- **Q3.** What do data protection, cookie and defamation law require?
- **Q4.** What does heritage law say about visiting sites, by nation?
- **Q5.** What do heritage bodies do about location sensitivity?

## Decision matrix

## Findings

### Online Safety Act 2023

- 2026-11-10, LAW: The Sch.1 para 4 "limited functionality" exemption covers only comments or reviews on provider content and reactions to them. User-created findings, photos and comments on other users' findings fell outside it, so the service was a regulated user-to-user service. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/1>.
- 2026-11-10, LAW: Every such service had s.9 (a suitable and sufficient illegal content risk assessment, kept up to date and repeated before significant changes), s.10 (proportionate measures and swift takedown once alerted, with terms saying how users are protected), s.20 (easy reporting for users and affected non-users) and s.21 (an accessible complaints procedure described in the terms). Ofcom could fine up to 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue or 18 million pounds. Same address.
- 2026-11-10, LAW: s.10(3A)-(3B), inserted 29 June 2026 by the Crime and Policing Act 2026, required systems to take down content reported as an intimate image, and substantially identical copies, "as soon as reasonably practicable, and no later than 48 hours" after the report. s.21(2A) added an expedited complaints route. A service accepting photo uploads needed this path. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/10>.
- 2026-11-10, GUIDANCE: Ofcom said "You have three months to complete a risk assessment after launching a service that the Act applies to". It expected a written record and a review at least annually. For a service assessed with good reason as low risk it expected "easy-to-find, understandable terms and conditions; a complaints tool ... backed up by a process to deal with those complaints; the ability to review content and take it down quickly ...; and a specific individual responsible for compliance". Ofcom said it was "not setting out to penalise small, low risk services trying to comply in good faith". Evidence: <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/helping-small-services-navigate-the-online-safety-act>.

### Children

- 2026-11-10, LAW and GUIDANCE: A child was anyone under 18. Every in-scope service had to complete a children's access assessment within three months of becoming available to UK users. Ofcom said a service could only conclude children cannot access it by using highly effective age assurance, and that a terms-of-service age limit did not count. Without that, stage two asked whether a significant number of children use the service or it is of a kind likely to attract them. If so, a children's risk assessment and children's safety duties applied. Evidence: <https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/childrens-access-assessment-duties-under-the-online-safety-act>.
- 2026-11-10, POLICY, not law: The government announced on 15 June 2026 an under-16 social media ban. The gov.uk fact sheet of 18 September 2026 said "The first set of Regulations will be laid before the end of the year, and the changes should be implemented in Spring 2027". It used the Australian model. Messaging services such as WhatsApp and "dedicated educational platforms" were not intended to be covered. Livestreaming and "strangers being able to contact children" were to be restricted on other online services, and 16 and 17 year olds were to get default-off features. Evidence: <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fact-sheet-new-rules-to-protect-children-online/fact-sheet-new-rules-to-protect-children-online>.
- 2026-11-10, GUIDANCE: Ofcom on 29 October 2026 named four age-assurance methods capable of being highly effective at 16. Whether a citizen-science mapping site counted as "social media" was not defined.
- 2026-11-10, LAW: UK GDPR Art.8 set 13 as the minimum age at which a child's own consent could be the lawful basis for an online service. New paras 2A-2B, inserted 29 April 2026 by the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, let the Secretary of State raise that as high as 16 by regulations. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/section/164A>.
- 2026-11-10: The research recommended contributor accounts for adults only, with read-only access open to all, until the under-16 regulations were published. It added that this would not remove the need for the children's access assessment. The project owner chose 16 instead, as Decision 0009 records.
- 2026-11-10: The research listed no private messaging, no livestreaming, moderated image uploads and restricted links for new accounts as ways to reduce assessed risk. It said image upload and any private messaging raised the risk and the duties that follow.

### Data protection, cookies and defamation

- 2026-11-10, LAW: Data Protection Act 2018 s.164A, fully in force 19 June 2026, required a controller to facilitate data-protection complaints, acknowledge within 30 days and respond without undue delay. Organisations using personal data had to pay the ICO data protection fee unless exempt. The ICO page was fetched, but fee tiers and the exemption test were not re-read. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/section/164A>.
- 2026-11-10: A user's contribution history could reveal where they live, so it was personal data. The research suggested pseudonymous display names. This was the research's reasoning and not a cited rule.
- 2026-11-10, LAW: PECR reg 6 was replaced on 5 February 2026 by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 and worked through Schedule A1. Para 4 exempted storage "strictly necessary for the provision of an information society service requested by the subscriber or user", with "automatically authenticating the identity of the subscriber or user" as an example. A login or guest session cookie therefore needed no banner. Para 5 allowed first-party statistics without consent if users got clear information and a simple free way to object and the data was not shared except to help improve the service. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/2426/schedule/A1>.
- 2026-11-10, LAW: Defamation Act 2013 s.5 (England and Wales) gave a website operator a defence that it did not post the statement. The defence was lost if the claimant could not identify the poster, gave a notice of complaint, and the operator did not follow the 2013 Regulations. Under SI 2013/3028 the operator had 48 hours to send the complaint to the poster and acknowledge the complainant. The poster had 5 days to respond. Where the operator had no means of contacting the poster, it "must, within 48 hours ... remove the statement". Moderating did not by itself lose the defence (s.5(12)). In Scotland, the Defamation and Malicious Publication (Scotland) Act 2021 s.3 restricted proceedings against secondary publishers. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3028/schedule/made>.
- 2026-11-10: The research said a marker on a field was not in itself personal data, but text or photos identifying an owner or occupier, or a feature inside a private garden, could be. This was reasoning, confidence "likely", with no evidence address. It named OSA s.20 reporting for affected non-users as the natural channel for landowners.

### Heritage law for field visits, by nation

| Nation | Law read | What it said | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| England | Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Areas Act 1979 | s.42(1): using a metal detector in a "protected place" without written consent of Historic England is an offence, fine to level 3. s.42(3): removing an object found with one is an offence, fine to the statutory maximum or unlimited on indictment. s.2: works on a scheduled monument without consent are an offence. s.28: intentional or reckless damage, up to two years on indictment | <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1979/46/section/42> |
| Wales | Historic Environment (Wales) Act 2023, Parts 2 to 7 in force 4 November 2024 (WSI 2024/860) | s.60: metal detector in a protected place without written consent of the Welsh Ministers, applied for through Cadw. s.58: damage. s.30(1): unauthorised works, per Cadw. Cadw said consent "will not normally be granted" outside a consented excavation or research strategy. The 1979 Act no longer applied, so user-facing text citing s.42 for Wales would have been wrong | <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asc/2023/3/section/60> |
| Scotland | 1979 Act s.42 with consent from Historic Environment Scotland. Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 s.9 | s.9 excluded from access rights being on land to commit an offence and "taking away, for commercial purposes or for profit, anything in or on the land". HES said "You must not metal detect on a scheduled monument or protected place - you could be fined 50,000 pounds or more and sent to prison". Landowner permission was needed everywhere | <https://www.outdooraccess-scotland.scot/sites/default/files/2018-05/Publication%202005%20-%20Scottish%20Outdoor%20Access%20Code.pdf> |
| Northern Ireland | Historic Monuments and Archaeological Objects (NI) Order 1995 | Art.41: excavating "for the purpose of searching generally for archaeological objects" without a Department licence is an offence, level 3, whether or not the surface is removed. Art.29: possessing a detecting device in a protected place without consent is an offence, level 4. Art.42: report finds within 14 days. DfC guidance said licences usually went to "bona fide archaeologists, historians or experienced amateurs", needed at least four weeks' notice, and about 300 were issued a year | <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1995/1625/article/41> |
| England and Wales, access land | Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 Sch.2 para 1 | The access right did not apply to a person who "(k) uses or has with him any metal detector", "(d) commits any criminal offence", or "(t) engages in any activity which is organised or undertaken ... for any commercial purpose". The research said most lowland earthworks sat on private farmland with no access right and that entry without permission was civil trespass. That last point came from general knowledge and was not fetched | <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/37/schedule/2> |

- 2026-11-10, GUIDANCE, Scotland: The Scottish Outdoor Access Code 2005 para 3.51 said to leave cultural heritage sites as found, with no digging or disturbing the ground and "camping, lighting fires or using metal detectors can lead to such disturbance". Para 3.64 said access rights extended to surveys of cultural heritage with a recreational or educational purpose. Same address as the Scotland row.
- 2026-11-10, LAW, finds: Treasure Act 1996 s.8 (England, Wales and NI) required a finder who believed an object was treasure to notify the coroner within 14 days, an offence otherwise (up to 3 months or a level 5 fine). The Treasure (Designation) (Amendment) Order 2023 (SI 2023/404) added objects at least 200 years old with any metal part that gave "exceptional insight" into national or regional history, for finds after about 30 July 2023. In Scotland all portable antiquities of significance were subject to Crown claim through Treasure Trove and had to be reported. Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/24/section/8>.
- 2026-11-10, LAW, records: Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023 s.230, requiring an English HER, was shown as "S. 230 not in force at Royal Assent". In Wales the HER duty was in force (Historic Environment (Wales) Act 2023 ss.194-195). Evidence: <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/55/section/230>.
- 2026-11-10: The research recommended a per-nation notice at the moment a visit is planned, a banner when a feature lies in a scheduled area, and the line "a marker is not permission to visit". It was a recommendation, not a duty found in law.

### Location sensitivity: practice and guidance

- 2026-11-10, PRACTICE: The PAS "Advice for finders" leaflet (PDF created 30 January 2024) said findspots "will not be identified more precisely than 1km and with the relevant parish or community name; the most sensitive findspots will not be identified as accurately as this". The leaflet was the only PAS source read. finds.org.uk returned HTTP 403 with "cf-mitigated: challenge", so current PAS terms, licence version and research-access tiers were not read first-hand. Evidence: <https://www.ncmd.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PAT-leaflet-FINAL.pdf>.
- 2026-11-10, LAW and PRACTICE: Environmental Information Regulations 2004 reg 12(5)(g) let a public authority refuse disclosure that would adversely affect "the protection of the environment to which the information relates". Heneb's access policy (updated 19 November 2024) said access would be denied if release "may endanger the archaeological resource", and bulk release could be restricted where it risked "the establishment of a competing and unregulated database". Evidence: <https://heneb.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/APPROVED-Heneb-HER-Access-and-Cost-Recovery-Policy2024-EN.pdf>.
- 2026-11-10, GUIDANCE: The Code of Practice for Responsible Metal Detecting in England and Wales was voluntary. Clause 7 said findspot information "should not be passed on to third parties without the agreement of the landowner/occupier". The text was read on a council mirror that cited the abolished MLA, so it was probably the 2006 edition. The 2017 revision could not be fetched. Evidence: <https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/information-and-resources/metal-detecting/code-of-practice-for-responsible-metal-detecting-in-england-and-wales>.
- 2026-11-10, GUIDANCE: The CIfA Code of conduct (revised October 2025) bound CIfA members only. It had no rule on publishing coordinates of unrecorded sites. Evidence: <https://www.archaeologists.net/sites/default/files/2025-10/Code-of-conduct_revOct-2025.pdf>.
- 2026-11-10: The research found no published rule from Historic England, ALGAO, Cadw or HES aimed at citizen platforms publishing coordinates of unrecorded sites. The UK HER Manual (khub.net/web/uk-her-manual) answered HTTP 200 but its navigation was script-rendered and was not parsed. Evidence: <https://khub.net/web/uk-her-manual>.
- 2026-11-10, PRACTICE: iNaturalist set the public point of an obscured record randomly within a 0.2 x 0.2 degree cell, about 500 km2, and kept true coordinates for trusted projects and curators. It documented its own leaks: clues in photos or descriptions, interpolation from the same user's nearby open observations, and exact dates. Evidence: <https://help.inaturalist.org/en/support/solutions/articles/151000169938-what-is-geoprivacy-what-does-it-mean-for-an-observation-to-be-obscured->.
- 2026-11-10: The research said obscuring needed to hold in vector tiles, API responses, search, bounding-box queries, exports, photo EXIF and timestamps, and against inference from a user's other contributions. This was reasoning with confidence "likely".
- 2026-11-10, PRACTICE: Cadw gave heritage-crime reporting as 999 if in progress, otherwise 101 quoting "Op Heritage Cymru", or Crimestoppers on 0800 555 111. Cadw said illegal metal detecting on a scheduled monument was an offence under s.30(1) of the Historic Environment (Wales) Act 2023. The English equivalent on historicengland.org.uk returned 403. Evidence: <https://cadw.gov.wales/advice-support/historic-assets/heritage-crime>.

## Outcome

The service was a regulated user-to-user service under the Online Safety Act, so it owed an illegal content risk assessment, a children's access assessment, reporting, complaints and takedown duties whatever its size. No law read barred 16 and 17 year olds from posting, and an age limit in the terms would not have removed the children's access assessment whether it was set at 16 or 18. Heritage law differed by nation and the Welsh and Northern Irish rules were easy to misstate. The open question was policy, not law. The under-16 regulations were due before the end of 2026 for spring 2027, and whether a citizen-science map counted as social media was undefined. The decision to admit 16 and 17 year olds was therefore lawful on the law read, with a review trigger when those regulations appear. Heritage-body guidance on sensitive locations rested on PAS, HER and iNaturalist practice, because Historic England, PAS and trove.scot could not be read by script.
