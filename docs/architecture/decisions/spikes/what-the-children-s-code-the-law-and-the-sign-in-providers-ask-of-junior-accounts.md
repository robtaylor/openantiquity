# Spike — What the children's code, the law and the sign-in providers ask of junior accounts

**Status:** Resolved (2026-11-14) — PARTIAL; junior accounts tied to a parent can go ahead in outline, with four changes to the plan, no OpenStreetMap sign-in for a junior, and six questions the sources leave open

## Question

Can 13 to 15 year olds be given accounts tied to a parent by an invite, as [the junior accounts plan](../../../plans/junior-accounts.md) designs them, within what the Information Commissioner's code, the law on a child's and a parent's agreement, and the sign-in providers' own terms allow?

## Why this is in question

Decision 0009 set the youngest contributor at 16. The research behind it, read on 2026-11-10 in [Platform duties and age](platform-duties-and-age.md), didn't cover three things the plan depends on: the Information Commissioner's code for services children are likely to use, whether a parent's invite is the agreement the law asks for, and whether a 13 year old may hold any of the three sign-ins at all.

This is a reading of sources, not legal advice. Where the sources leave a question open, it is listed as open.

## Approach

- **Q1.** What does the Children's code ask of a service that knowingly admits 13 to 15 year olds?
  - Step 1: Read the code's standards and the guidance on who it applies to, on ico.org.uk.
  - Step 2: Set each against the plan.
- **Q2.** Is a parent's invite the agreement the law asks for, and what would be needed below 13?
  - Step 1: Read UK GDPR Article 8 as in force on legislation.gov.uk, and the Commissioner's guidance on children.
  - Step 2: Find what has been laid or made of the under-16 rules as of 14 November 2026.
- **Q3.** Which sign-ins may a 13 year old hold?
  - Step 1: Read each provider's own terms for its minimum age in the UK.

## Findings

All read on 2026-11-14. Quotations are the sources' own words.

### The Children's code

- The code applies to "information society services likely to be accessed by children". The Commissioner's guidance treats a service that "allows users under the age of 18" as offered directly to children, which the site already does at 16. Whether a free, non-commercial site is an information society service is open: the test is economic activity of a kind "typically provided on a commercial basis".
- Since 5 February 2026 Article 25(1A) of the UK GDPR has made the "children's higher protection matters" part of the law for such services, and not only guidance.
- A data protection impact assessment "must" be done "before the service is launched", covering each standard and the lawful basis. The plan scheduled the Online Safety Act's risk assessment and no such assessment.
- The code names "account holder confirmation" among the ways to establish age, where "an existing account holder who you know to be an adult" confirms a child's age. That supports the invite. Under the plan the parent's own age is only declared.
- Where a parent can monitor a child, the code asks for "a clear and obvious sign for the child (such as a lit up icon) which lets them know when monitoring or tracking is active", and for materials for the child and for the parent on the child's right to privacy. The plan told both once, when the account was made.
- Privacy information has to be written for the age of the child, with a version for parents and short notices at the point where data is used.
- Settings must be "high privacy" by default. That reaches anything that makes a junior's data visible to others, and a finding is a place the child marked. The Commissioner says it "will look very carefully" at a claim that something is part of the core service.
- The code neither allows nor forbids a parent reading a child's private notes. It leaves the balance to the assessment.

### A parent's agreement

- Article 8 sets 13 as the age at which a child gives their own consent to an online service. It applies only "where point (a) of Article 6(1) applies", which is when consent is the lawful basis.
- So at 13 to 15 the law doesn't ask for a parent's agreement on any basis. The tie to a parent is a safeguard the site chooses, not a legal requirement.
- The site's privacy notice gives purposes and names no lawful basis. Which basis is chosen decides whether Article 8 applies at all. The Commissioner's guidance says consent "is unlikely to be the most appropriate basis for processing which is necessary to deliver the core service", that a contract with a child is generally voidable in England and Wales, and that legitimate interests can be relied on with "extra care".
- Below 13, where consent is the basis, the holder of parental responsibility must give it and the service must make "reasonable efforts" to verify that. An invite link with nothing checked about the relationship wasn't tested against that, since the plan stops at 13.
- The earlier spike's link for Article 8 points to section 164A of the Data Protection Act 2018, which is a different section, and section 9 of that Act was removed in 2020. The article itself is the source.

### The rules announced for under-16s, on 14 November 2026

- Section 214A of the Online Safety Act 2023, inserted on 29 April 2026, is a power to make regulations and no more. Among the features it names are those by which "a person who is not known to a user of the service could identify the actual or approximate location of the user", and unsolicited contact from a stranger.
- No regulations under it were found on legislation.gov.uk, in draft or made.
- The government's response of July 2026, updated on 19 August, said: "We intend to lay the first set of regulations before Parliament by the end of this year and expect the restrictions on social media services offering their services to under-16s to enter into force in spring 2027."
- On the age of consent it said: "We will not proceed at this stage with a change to the age of consent."
- The same response said children "will still be able to access educational, informational and creative online spaces".

### The sign-ins

- **OpenStreetMap.** Its terms say "By registering as a user ... you represent that you are at least 16 years old". At 13 to 15 "a parent or other adult authorized by your parent may create an account for you to use", which "must not contain any personal data about you". A junior can't hold an OpenStreetMap sign-in of their own.
- **Google.** 13 is the age to manage one's own account in the UK.
- **The emailed link.** Gmail, Outlook and iCloud Mail each set 13 in the UK.

### Not read

- parliament.uk refused automated access, so the House of Commons Library's briefing and the Hansard debate of 2 November weren't read.
- Four of the code's standards were read only in their headlines: 5, 6, 14 and 15.
- Microsoft's and Apple's full terms, and what a supervised Google account may sign in to on another site.

## Outcome

The outline stands: a parent vouching for a child is a method the Children's code names, and a 13 year old may hold a Google sign-in or an emailed link. Four things in the plan must change: a data protection impact assessment before launch, a lasting sign to the child that a parent can see what they do, privacy information written for 13 to 15 year olds and for parents, and high-privacy defaults that reach beyond the profile to what is public about a junior's findings. OpenStreetMap can't be a junior's sign-in. Six questions stay open for the owner and for advice: the lawful basis, whether the site is an information society service, how sure the site must be that the inviting adult is an adult, whether a parent may read private notebooks, whether a junior's published work can stay after they ask for it to be erased, and whether the rules for 2027 will reach a map of places a child marked.
